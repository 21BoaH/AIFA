01 — Summary

Abstract

The AI Fitness Assistant is a browser-based computer vision system that tracks resistance-training exercises in real time from an ordinary webcam or phone camera. Google’s MediaPipe Pose model locates 33 body landmarks in every frame; the application converts them into joint angles and body positions, smooths the signal, and runs a per-exercise state machine that only counts a repetition once its full range of motion is complete.

The Exercise Lab ships 23 trackers: 21 repetition counters and 2 timed holds. Form checks turn the on-screen skeleton red and explain the fault in plain words. All inference runs on the user’s device, so no video or body data ever leaves the browser. In automated testing the current engine handled 33 of 33 scripted scenarios correctly on both 16:9 and 4:3 cameras, against 20 of 33 for the previous version.

02 — Context

Problem statement & motivation

Resistance exercise provides proven cardiovascular, metabolic and musculoskeletal health benefits. However, inexperienced lifters face substantial risks of joint strain and acute injury when they train with poor posture or a compromised range of motion, and most train without a coach watching.

Wearable inertial measurement units (IMUs) and motion-capture suits can measure movement precisely, but they need specialised, expensive hardware. This project investigates whether a consumer webcam and edge machine learning can deliver useful biomechanical feedback directly in a web browser.

The aim is not to replace a coach. It is to answer, immediately and objectively, the two questions every lifter asks: did that rep count? and was my position safe?

03 — Scope

Objectives & requirements

Functional

  • Track the body in real time from a single RGB camera (webcam or phone).
  • Count a repetition only when the full range of motion is reached, ignoring half-reps and bounces.
  • Time isometric holds (Plank, Wall Sit) only while the position is actually held.
  • Detect common form faults and explain them in words.
  • Cover upper body, lower body, core and full-body movements.

Non-functional

  • Private: all processing on-device; no account and no uploads.
  • Zero install: a static website that runs in any modern browser.
  • Camera-independent: the same movement measures the same on any camera shape and at any distance.
  • Accessible: keyboard-operable controls, visible focus, and cues announced to screen readers.

Lifecycle of a set

  1. Start opens the camera and, the first time only, creates the pose model. Later Starts reuse the same model and call reset(): MediaPipe’s WebAssembly runtime cannot be rebuilt on a page, so closing and re-creating it would leave the tracker silent.
  2. Each frame is sent to the model. Its results arrive in onResults, which measures the body, advances the state machine and redraws the skeleton.
  3. Stop ends the set: the camera turns off and the rep count, hold timer, smoothing history and form state all reset to zero. A frame still being processed when Stop is pressed is ignored, so it cannot leave stale state for the next set.
05 — Input

Pose landmarks

MediaPipe Pose, based on the BlazePose network [1], returns 33 landmarks per frame. Each has an x and y position as a fraction of the frame (0–1), a relative depth z, and a visibility score. The trackers run the full model (complexity 1) with landmark smoothing on and detection and tracking confidence set to 0.7.

A landmark is only trusted when its visibility is at least 0.5, and an angle is only measured when all three of its points pass. A limb hidden from the camera therefore drops out instead of producing a wild reading.

06 — Geometry

Measuring joint angles

A joint angle uses three landmarks: for the elbow, the shoulder (A), elbow (B) and wrist (C). The two-argument arctangent gives the direction of each limb segment, and their difference is the angle at B.

Landmark x is a fraction of the frame’s width and y of its height. On a 16:9 webcam one unit of x is 1.78 times longer than one unit of y, so using the raw numbers skews every angle that isn’t perfectly horizontal or vertical. The trackers rescale x by the video’s aspect ratio first:

const aspect = video.videoWidth / video.videoHeight; let radians = Math.atan2(c.y - b.y, (c.x - b.x) * aspect) - Math.atan2(a.y - b.y, (a.x - b.x) * aspect); let angle = Math.abs(radians * 180 / Math.PI); if (angle > 180) angle = 360 - angle;

For the squat the spread across frame shapes falls from 14.5° uncorrected to 8.3° corrected. MediaPipe’s 3D world landmarks [3] were the most stable (3.5°) but read a straight leg as about 168°, too close to the 160–165° lockout thresholds. The trackers therefore use aspect-corrected 2D angles, which keep the thresholds’ meaning.

Distance-independent measures

Some movements are judged by position rather than angle. Each is scaled by the body itself, so the result doesn’t change with distance from the camera:

  • Lat Pulldown: wrist height above or below the shoulders, measured in shoulder widths.
  • Calf Raises: heel height above the toes as a fraction of shin length, compared with a resting baseline captured at Start.
  • Pull-ups: head height compared with the hands, which mark the bar.
  • Plank, Burpees, torso-lean checks: torso tilt measured in true pixel proportions.
07 — Signal

Smoothing the signal

Landmarks jitter slightly from frame to frame. Each reading is averaged over the last ~160 ms (at most five frames). A fixed five-frame average, used previously, lags more as the frame rate drops. On a slower phone it can smooth away the bottom of a fast rep before the angle crosses its threshold.

The table shows a simulated squat rep (knee 170° → 85° → 170°) and the lowest smoothed angle each method reaches. The rep counts only if that value drops below the 100° threshold.

08 — Logic

Counting reps

Each tracker is a finite state machine with two thresholds and a gap between them. For a squat, the knee angle dropping below 100° arms the rep (DOWN), and rising above 160° while armed counts it (UP). The gap stops jitter around a single line from double-counting, and the required order means half-reps and bounces never count.

STARTknee < 100° DOWNknee > 160° UP · rep + 1

10 — Experience

Feedback & interface

On the camera view

  • While a set runs, only the skeleton and the rep progress meter are drawn over the video. There are no numbers or letters on the body.
  • The skeleton turns red while a form check fails.
  • The progress meter fills from 0% at the starting position to 100% at the end of the movement. Timed holds don’t show it.
  • The canvas is sized to the camera frame and cropped exactly like the video, so the skeleton sits on the body.

In the side panel

  • The rep count (or hold time in seconds), reset to zero by Stop.
  • The next cue, colour-coded: amber waiting, volt act now, red fix your form.
  • Form messages such as “KEEP YOUR CHEST UP”, “KEEP YOUR TORSO UPRIGHT” or “HINGE AT THE HIPS, NOT THE KNEES”.
  • The count and cues are live regions, so screen readers announce them.

Controls: Start, Stop, Flip Camera (front or rear, with mirroring handled) and Hide/Show Skeleton.

11 — Trust

Privacy & performance

Privacy

Inference runs in the browser on WebGL and WebAssembly. No video, image or landmark data is sent anywhere: the only network requests are for the site itself, its fonts and the MediaPipe model files. There are no accounts and no analytics.

Performance

  • One pose model per page, reused across sets.
  • Only the skeleton is drawn each frame; the camera image is never redrawn onto the canvas.
  • Smoothing adapts to the device’s frame rate.
  • The full model (complexity 1) balances accuracy and speed.
12 — Testing

Evaluation

  1. Synthetic skeletons. An automated browser harness replaces the pose model with skeletons of exactly known joint angles and runs each tracker’s own page code. There are 33 scenarios covering all 23 trackers:
    • 16:9 and 4:3 cameras;
    • a person close to the camera and twice as far;
    • fast reps at about 9 fps;
    • movements that must not count (curls on the press and tricep pages, squatting during an RDL);
    • form faults, timed holds, and Stop/Start resets.
  2. Real model. The real MediaPipe Pose package ran against a fake webcam feed of photographs. Every tracker went through three Start/Stop sessions.
  3. Measurement study. The angle and smoothing comparisons in sections 06 and 07.

The 13 scenarios the previous engine failed exposed real bugs. On a 16:9 camera Deadlift, Romanian Deadlift and Wall Sit never registered. Pull-ups could not count from a dead hang, and Shoulder Press counted on the way down. Curls added reps to Shoulder Press and Overhead Tricep Extension, and Lat Pulldown depended on distance from the camera. Fast reps were missed at low frame rates.

These tests prove each tracker does what its rules say. They do not prove the thresholds suit every body: no study with real athletes has been run yet (see section 13).

13 — Outlook

Limitations & future work

Known limitations

  • Monocular depth ambiguity: a single 2D camera loses accuracy when a limb moves towards or away from it, which is why most trackers ask for a side-on view.
  • Self-occlusion: overlapping limbs lower the model’s confidence, and a hidden joint drops out of the measurement.
  • Lighting and background: dim light or a busy background adds landmark jitter.
  • Heuristic thresholds: the angles come from common coaching cues, not from a measured population, and may need tuning for mobility or body proportions.
  • One person in frame: other people in view can confuse the model.

Future work

  • A validation study with recorded, hand-labelled reps from real athletes.
  • Optional 3D world-landmark angles, with thresholds re-tuned on that dataset.
  • Per-user calibration of range of motion.
  • Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) for continuous phase prediction, and dual-camera fusion.
  • Per-set statistics such as tempo and depth, and adaptive filters such as the 1€ filter [5].
